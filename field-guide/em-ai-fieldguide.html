The whole idea in three steps

Entrepreneurial mindset is made from 18 habits, each formed through repeated practice. AI can deepen that practice or quietly erase it. The 18 habits of entrepreneurial mindset are built, not taught. AI is now the biggest force acting on how they get built.

What this is A working guide to practicing 18 entrepreneurial mindset habits in an age of AI, for your own work and your classroom. Every habit is shown working in real engineering case studies.
How to use it Read the idea below, then pick a habit that matters to you. Each one shows how AI helps, the trap to avoid, a prompt to run yourself, and an activity to run with students.
What you'll leave with One habit worth practicing this week, a prompt you can use today, and a class activity you could run next.
1

Habits form through practice

Curiosity, systems thinking, value creation. None of these are facts you can teach in a lecture. They are habits, and habits are built by doing the thinking yourself, repeatedly.

2

AI short-circuits practice by default

Every student and every faculty member now has AI in their pocket. Used naively, it hands over clean answers and the thinking never happens. Each habit has a specific failure mode, an anti-pattern, where AI quietly does the habit for you.

3

The right prompt flips the relationship

Each prompt in this guide turns AI into a sparring partner for one specific habit. Instead of AI being curious for you, the prompt makes you practice being curious. Every habit comes in two modes. Run it yourself in your own work, or run it with students as a classroom activity.

The master principle

You can't evaluate work in a domain you haven't mastered, so polished output reads as competent when it isn't. AI amplifies the judgment you bring. It cannot supply the judgment you lack. Develop expertise first, then let AI scale it. Never the reverse.

The disposition underneath
The psychology under the 18 habits

The disposition underneath

Eighteen habits, fifty frameworks, and a gap between them that this guide should say out loud. A framework is a procedure you can perform once. A habit is what you do without deciding to. Running a Duncker diagram does not make you a contrarian thinker, and one after-action review does not make anyone persistent.

The frameworks are scaffolding, not the building

Every framework here exists because some disposition is hard to sustain unaided. The pre-mortem exists because optimism bias is real and asking people to predict failure does not work. Retrieval practice exists because rereading feels like learning and is not. Read that way, a framework is a prosthetic for a disposition you have not built yet - useful immediately, and still useful later, but not the same thing as having the habit. Nobody becomes curious by owning a list of questions.

The obstacle is specific to each habit, and AI moves it the wrong way

There is a pattern across all eighteen. Each habit has a particular cognitive obstacle standing in its way, and in nearly every case AI lowers the cost of doing exactly what the obstacle already wanted you to do. Confirmation bias meets a model tuned to agree. Need for closure meets an unlimited supply of confident answers. The illusion of explanatory depth meets fluent summaries. Subtraction neglect meets a system that proposes additions by default. This is the guide's argument about instruments and replacements, restated at the level of the person: the danger is not that AI does the work badly, it is that it does the biased move faster and more pleasantly than you would have done it alone.

What actually forms a habit

Dispositions move through repetition, in a specific context, with feedback, over time - not through reading. Two things reliably help. The first is attaching the practice to a cue rather than to an intention: not "I will be more skeptical" but "when I am about to accept an AI answer, I will ask what would make it wrong." A plan tied to a situation survives contact with a busy week; a resolution does not. The second is accepting that the practice which feels worst often teaches most - effortful retrieval, spaced returns, mixed problem types. If an exercise in this guide feels inefficient, that is frequently the exercise working.

Which is why the teaching mode matters more than the prompts

A prompt read once forms nothing. The Run it with students side of every habit is the only part of this guide that repeats - across a term, with feedback, with consequences attached - and repetition with feedback is the whole mechanism. If you take one thing from these pages into a course, take the assignment structure rather than the prompt. The prompt is a demonstration. The course is where a habit could actually form.

A note on the psychology we are not using

Some of the best-known ideas in this space have not held up, and a guide for faculty should not lean on them. Growth mindset interventions produce small effects that have replicated poorly at scale. Grit adds little to what conscientiousness already explains. Learning styles have been tested repeatedly and do not work. The ten-thousand-hour rule is a popularisation that Ericsson, whose research it came from, spent years disputing. Everything named on these pages was chosen because it is well established and because it earns its place - and where a framework has a known flaw, such as the rank reversals produced by multiplying risk priority numbers, this guide says so rather than passing it on.

New to MCP? Here's the short version

The Model Context Protocol is an open standard that lets an AI assistant connect to an outside server and pull in extra material. Instead of pasting a prompt from this site every time, you connect the server once and can ask for any habit or workflow by name.

✓ Read-only and stateless. It only ever returns text, prompts and reference content.
✓ No login, no account, nothing to install. One address to paste.
✓ Stores no data and cannot modify files, send messages, or act on your behalf.

The case studies

The habits in this guide are not hypothetical: they show up, on camera, in real engineering work. Seven case studies, coded against the 18 habits segment by segment. Start anywhere.

An honest note on the evidence. Seven case studies were coded against the 18 habits, segment by segment. In that coded evidence some habits are everywhere and some are thin: Socially Minded rests on a single Skycam moment, and Knowledge Synthesis and Impact Thinking each appear in only two of the seven cases. The iFLY clips shown beside each habit are illustrative picks from the interviews; the coded iFLY corpus supports ten habits, and for four others no interview question could have surfaced the habit at all — an absence of the instrument, not of the engineers. One negative finding is worth teaching on its own: across 65 iFLY clips about a customer-experience business, nobody describes learning anything from an actual customer.
